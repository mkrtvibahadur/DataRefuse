The Consent and
Data Refusal Audit.

We examine whether digital platforms give people meaningful, accessible and reversible control over how their personal data is collected and used.

THE CENTRAL PROPOSITION

A privacy policy is not the same as a meaningful choice.

A platform can publish privacy controls while making refusal difficult to find, fragmented across settings, poorly explained or costly to exercise. This audit examines the architecture of refusal — not simply whether a privacy policy exists.

KEY FINDING

Reversibility outranks refusal.

Platforms score better on letting users change a choice inside an account than on letting them refuse optional data use before or at the point of collection. Refusal accessibility has the lowest mean score: 0.6 out of 2.

METHOD & LIMITATIONS

A focused interface audit, not a compliance verdict.

One logged-out session, India (Delhi egress), English, one Chrome-family browser, 13 November 2026. Interfaces can vary by account, location, device and A/B testing. Account-level controls were not tested; information from official documentation is distinguished from controls directly observed in the interface. Scores are exploratory researcher judgements about refusal architecture, not measurements of legal compliance. Where possible, refusal actions were performed and re-verified.

Conceptual frame: privacy self-management (Solove, 2013); contextual integrity (Nissenbaum, 2004); choice architecture (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008); consent and dark patterns (Utz et al., 2019; Nouwens et al., 2020); DPDP Act 2023, sections 6 and 11–13.
